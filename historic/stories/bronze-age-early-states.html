Bronze Age & Early States

Mesopotamia · Egypt · Indus · Aegean · China · beyond

Bronze Age worlds invent writing in several places, concentrate surplus, and build temples and palaces that archive gods and grain in the same buildings. “Civilization” here means paperwork and inequality as much as pyramids.

Chapter 1 of 4

Metal, labor, and long roads

Bronze needs copper and tin — often from distant sources — so metallurgy pulls trade and diplomacy into everyday elite life. Weapons and tools matter; so do prestige objects that say who may speak for a city.

Not every Bronze Age society is a territorial empire. Palace centers, city leagues, and village hinterlands coexist.

Chapter 2 of 4

Temples as banks and theaters

In southern Mesopotamia, temple and palace households manage land, rations, and cult. Gods receive offerings that also feed dependents — religion as accounting.

Egyptian divine kingship and Indus urban planning (with still-undeciphered script) show alternate solutions to order at scale.

Chapter 3 of 4

Writing changes what can be remembered

Cuneiform, hieroglyphs, Linear B, early Chinese graphs: each encodes different genres — law, ritual, inventory, royal boast. Literacy is usually a scribal craft, not a mass skill.

Oral cultures remain sophisticated; writing mainly freezes claims that elites want durable.

Chapter 4 of 4

Crisis and the end of an archaeological label

Around 1200 BCE eastern Mediterranean palace systems fracture — drought, migration, war, and systems failure all appear in the debate. Calling it “collapse” can hide continuity in villages and new Iron Age configurations.

Why it still matters: early states normalize bureaucracy, sacred kingship, and inequality as “civilization” — themes later ages inherit and argue with.

Further reading vibe

Van de Mieroop on the Near East; Kenoyer on Indus; Shelmerdine on the Aegean; Bagley on early China. Compare collapse narratives carefully — 1200 BCE is not one global switch.
