Ibn Sīnā

Persianate world · Bukhara to Hamadan · Avicenna and the Canon of Medicine

Abū ʿAlī ibn Sīnā (Avicenna) wrote philosophy and medicine that shaped curricula from Isfahan to Montpellier. The Canon of Medicine organized diagnosis and pharmacology so effectively that Latin Europe kept teaching it into the early modern era.

Categories: Thinkers · Science & invention

Chapter 1 of 6

Polymath youth

Born near Bukhara, he claimed vast early reading in logic, metaphysics, and medicine. Court service and restless travel followed political upheavals.

Chapter 2 of 6

The Canon

Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb synthesized Galenic tradition with clinical observation — anatomy, diseases, compound drugs — in a teachable structure.

Chapter 3 of 6

Philosophy

His metaphysics and psychology influenced Islamic falsafa and, through Latin translation, medieval Christian scholasticism — sometimes as a sparring partner.

Chapter 4 of 6

Clinic and experiment

Stories of bedside skill abound; more important is his insistence on testing remedies and describing contagious disease carefully for his time.

Chapter 5 of 6

Latin afterlife

Translated Avicenna became a university staple. European medicine’s Arab authorities were often Persian and Andalusian scholars writing in Arabic.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

Ibn Sīnā embodies a cosmopolitan scientific culture that Europe inherited rather than invented from nothing.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.
