September 1797

Hermione Mutiny

Caribbean · HMS Hermione · Bloodiest mutiny in the Royal Navy

On the night of 21–22 September 1797, sailors on HMS Hermione murdered Captain Hugh Pigot and most of the officers, then sailed the frigate to Spanish La Guaira. It was the bloodiest mutiny in British naval history — distinct from the Bounty.

Categories: Free zones & odd states

Chapter 1 of 6

Pigot’s terror

Pigot was infamous for arbitrary flogging. In a navy already shaken by the Spithead and Nore mutinies of 1797, Hermione’s crew reached breaking point.

Chapter 2 of 6

Night of knives

Mutineers stormed the cabin, killed Pigot and other officers, and threw bodies overboard. Chaos, drink, and revenge mixed; a core group drove the violence.

Chapter 3 of 6

To the enemy

Fearing the noose, they delivered the ship to Spain. Hermione became Santa Cecilia; some mutineers scattered across the Caribbean and Atlantic.

Chapter 4 of 6

Hunt and hanging

The Royal Navy hunted survivors for years. Courts-martial ended in hangings; the cut-out of the ship by HMS Surprise in 1799 became legend.

Chapter 5 of 6

Asylum echoes

Cases of alleged American sailors among mutineers fed U.S. debates on impressment, extradition, and political asylum under John Adams.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

Hermione is a window onto naval violence, class, and the thin line between discipline and tyranny at sea.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.
