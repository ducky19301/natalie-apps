c. 4 BCE – 65 CE · Rome

Seneca the Younger

Statesman, tutor to Nero, and the Stoic whose letters still circulate as practical philosophy. He wrote on anger, adversity, and the scarcity of time — then was ordered to die by the emperor he once advised. Still here: essays on managing emotion and using time well echo in modern self-help and leadership writing; Stoic ideas he helped popularize later informed cognitive approaches to distress.

c. 50 – 135 CE · Stoic bridge

Epictetus

Born enslaved, later a teacher whose students preserved the Enchiridion. He sharpened a rule Seneca’s school lived by: some things are up to us — judgments, choices — and some are not. Still here: “We are disturbed not by events, but by our judgments about them” (paraphrase of Enchiridion 5) became a cornerstone of REBT and CBT — therapies used worldwide today.

551 – 479 BCE · China

Confucius

Teacher and editor of rites and poetry whose sayings were gathered in the Analects. He tied personal virtue (ren, humaneness) to learning, family roles, and good government — character as the root of public order. Still here: exam-based civil service ideals, respect for teachers, and ethics of duty and relationship still shape East Asian education and public life — and influence global debates on character education.

1759 – 1797 · Britain

Mary Wollstonecraft

Writer and philosopher who answered Rousseau’s limited schooling for girls with a sharper claim: women are rational moral agents. In A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) she demanded serious education and civic standing — not ornament. Still here: coeducation, women’s suffrage movements, and modern human-rights arguments for equal schooling all draw on the line she drew between reason, virtue, and equal opportunity.

c. 965 – 1040 · Cairo

Ibn al-Haytham

Known in Latin as Alhazen, he wrote the seven-volume Book of Optics. He rejected the old idea that eyes emit rays, argued light enters the eye, described the camera obscura, and insisted on controlled experiments — not authority alone. Still here: intromission vision theory, optical science behind lenses and cameras, and the habit of testing hypotheses experimentally — a deep root of the modern scientific method.
