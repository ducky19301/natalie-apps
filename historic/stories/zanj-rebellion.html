869–883 CE

Zanj Rebellion

Southern Iraq · marshlands near Basra · Enslaved revolt that shook the Abbasids

The Zanj Rebellion was one of the largest enslaved uprisings in Islamic history. Under the leadership of ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad, laborers in the salt marshes of lower Iraq seized towns, defeated caliphal armies, and founded a capital before a grinding reconquest.

Categories: Africa & diaspora

Chapter 1 of 6

Marshes and misery

Large gangs of East African and other enslaved workers cleared nitrous soils around Basra for plantation agriculture. Conditions were brutal; Arabic sources call them Zanj.

Chapter 2 of 6

A prophet of revolt

ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad preached justice and raised a multi-ethnic following — enslaved people, peasants, and some Bedouin. Ideology mixed egalitarian claims with apocalyptic politics.

Chapter 3 of 6

Cities fall

Rebels took Ubulla and threatened Basra; caliphal prestige cracked. They built al-Mukhtāra (the Chosen) as a fortified capital in the marshes.

Chapter 4 of 6

Fourteen years

From 869 to 883 the war drained Abbasid resources already strained by other crises. Atrocities on both sides fill the chronicles; this was no brief riot.

Chapter 5 of 6

Reconquest

Al-Muwaffaq’s campaigns finally crushed the revolt. Leaders were killed; the marshes were retaken. The plantation system was damaged but empire survived.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

The Zanj Rebellion belongs in any global history of slavery and resistance — beside Haiti, Bahia, and Jamaica — and complicates simplistic pictures of a slavery-free Islamic world.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.
