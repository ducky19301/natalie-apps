Paleolithic & Neolithic Spiritual Life

Overview · caves, burials, figurines, first villages

Before temples and scriptures, people still marked death, animals, places, and seasons. The evidence is fragmentary — ochre, graves, cave walls, house floors — so every reconstruction is provisional. What we can say carefully still overturns the caricature of “primitive” minds.

Chapter 1 of 4

Burials and care for the dead

Intentional burial with ochre, tools, or ornaments appears in Upper Paleolithic contexts; Neanderthal burial claims remain contested case by case. Graves do not prove a single doctrine of afterlife; they do show social investment in bodies and memory.

Later Neolithic cemeteries and skull curation (as at Jericho or Çatalhöyük) suggest ancestor presence in domestic space — practices more than creeds.

Chapter 2 of 4

Images without captions

Franco-Cantabrian cave art and portable figurines invite shamanic, hunting-magic, and social theories. None is proven as a universal key. What is clear: some communities repeatedly entered difficult spaces to paint animals, signs, and hybrid figures.

“Venus” figurines are diverse; reading them all as fertility goddesses flattens regional difference. Better to ask what materials, bodies, and workshops local groups chose.

Chapter 3 of 4

From camps to houses that remember

Neolithic sedentism concentrated ritual in houses, plazas, and landscape monuments. Göbekli Tepe’s carved pillars sit in a still-debated pre- or early-farming context. Agriculture did not invent religion; it changed its architecture and labor.

Megaliths, enclosures, and alignments show calendrical and communal investment — sky watching as social technology, not yet Hellenistic horoscopes.

Chapter 4 of 4

What we should not claim

We lack Paleolithic scriptures. Projecting later mythologies backward is storytelling, not method. Comparative ethnography can suggest analogies, never proofs.

Why it still matters: deep human history of ritual undercuts both “religion begins with empires” and “only written faiths count.”

Absence of texts is not absence of meaning — only of our preferred evidence.

Further reading vibe

Clottes on cave art; Insoll on archaeology of ritual; Hodder on Çatalhöyük. Avoid single “goddess cult” grand theories that outrun the data.
