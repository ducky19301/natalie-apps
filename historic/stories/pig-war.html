The Pig War

San Juan Island · Pacific Northwest · When one pig nearly started a US–British war

In June 1859 an American settler on San Juan Island shot a British company’s pig rooting in his garden. Soldiers followed. For months the United States and Britain faced off over an island chain — without firing on each other.

Categories: Free zones & odd states

Chapter 1 of 6

A fuzzy treaty

The 1846 Oregon Treaty set the boundary along the middle of the channel between Vancouver Island and the mainland but left unclear which channel — Haro or Rosario — ran through the San Juans.

Chapter 2 of 6

Lyman Cutlar’s pig

Hudson’s Bay Company sheep ran on the island; American squatters claimed land. Cutlar killed a company boar; demands for damages escalated into flags and troops.

Chapter 3 of 6

Generals and gunboats

Captain George Pickett landed U.S. infantry; British warships arrived. Cooler heads in both capitals ordered no shooting. Joint military occupation began.

Chapter 4 of 6

Twelve quiet years

American and British camps coexisted with surprising civility — shared celebrations, monitored tempers. The war was a crisis managed by restraint.

Chapter 5 of 6

Kaiser’s map

Arbitration by Kaiser Wilhelm I in 1872 awarded the islands to the United States via the Haro Strait line. The British withdrew.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

National parks now interpret both camps. The Pig War is a parable of ambiguous wording, imperial pride, and the value of not pulling the trigger.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.
