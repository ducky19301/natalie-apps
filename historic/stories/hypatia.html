Hypatia

Alexandria · Roman Egypt · Mathematician of late antique Alexandria

Hypatia headed a philosophical circle in Alexandria, edited astronomical and mathematical works, and advised officials. Her murder in 415 CE made her a martyr for later ages — sometimes more symbol than the careful scholar the sources describe.

Categories: Thinkers · Women written out · Science & invention

Chapter 1 of 6

Daughter of Theon

Trained by her father Theon, a known mathematician associated with the Mouseion tradition, she surpassed him as a teacher of philosophy and exact sciences.

Chapter 2 of 6

Commentary culture

Late antique science often meant careful commentary. Hypatia is linked to work on Diophantus, Apollonius, and Ptolemaic astronomy — the infrastructure of learning.

Chapter 3 of 6

Public philosopher

Students included pagans and Christians. Synesius of Cyrene’s letters show affection and respect. She moved in civic networks, not a cloister.

Chapter 4 of 6

City on edge

Alexandria’s bishop Cyril clashed with the prefect Orestes. Street violence between factions turned philosophical fame into political vulnerability.

Chapter 5 of 6

The killing

A Christian mob seized her, dragged her to a church, and murdered her with tiles or shells — accounts vary in detail, not in horror. Ancient sources blame political hatred clothed in piety.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

Hypatia’s afterlife — Enlightenment heroine, feminist icon, cautionary tale — shows how we use the past. The historical woman remains a rare named female scientist of antiquity.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.
