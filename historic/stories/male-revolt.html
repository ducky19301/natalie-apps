January 1835

Malê Revolt

Salvador · Bahia · Empire of Brazil · Muslim uprising in Bahia, 1835

On the night of 24–25 January 1835, African Muslims called malês in Bahia rose in Salvador. Planned around Ramadan and a Sunday rest day, the revolt was forced early by betrayal, then crushed — but it terrified slaveholding society across the Atlantic.

Categories: Africa & diaspora

Chapter 1 of 6

Malês in Bahia

Nagô (Yoruba) and other West African Muslims formed literate, organized communities among the enslaved and freed. Islam offered dignity, schooling, and networks.

Chapter 2 of 6

Plan and betrayal

Leaders timed the rising for late Ramadan. A tip-off brought police into a meeting house; fighting erupted before the full plan matured.

Chapter 3 of 6

Hours on the streets

Hundreds fought toward military targets and tried to free jailed leaders. They avoided random massacre of planter families — a disciplined political revolt, not chaos.

Chapter 4 of 6

Defeat

Better-armed National Guard, police, and civilians prevailed by morning. Dozens of Africans died in combat; hundreds faced courts.

Chapter 5 of 6

Punishments and deportations

Whippings, prison, executions (few after appeals), and mass deportations to West Africa followed. Arabic papers and amulets became evidence.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

Historian João José Reis and others placed the Malê Revolt at the center of Atlantic slavery studies — a Black Muslim modernity under the lash.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.

Further reading vibe

João José Reis, Slave Rebellion in Brazil; Smithsonian Folklife essays; Bahian archival trial records.
