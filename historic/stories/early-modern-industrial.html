c. 1450–1914 (overlapping revolutions)

Early Modern to Industrial

Global connections · empires · machines

Printing, ocean empires, scientific societies, Atlantic slavery, and fossil-fueled industry remake pace and scale. “Progress” narratives forget that the same centuries intensify extraction and racial hierarchies.

Shelves: Ages & eras

Chapter 1 of 4

Oceans as new highways

Iberian, then Dutch, English, and French maritime empires move silver, sugar, enslaved people, and microbes. Indigenous American and African histories are central, not footnotes.

Early modern is also Ottoman, Mughal, Ming/Qing, Safavid — multipolar until industrial-military gaps widen.

Chapter 2 of 4

Print, science, and contested authority

Print multiplies Bibles, pamphlets, and star charts. Experimental philosophy challenges university Aristotelianism without instantly secularizing Europe.

Astrology and astronomy still share practitioners; Newton’s gravity sits beside his private alchemical and prophetic studies.

Chapter 3 of 4

Industrial acceleration

Coal, steam, factories, and later electricity compress time and expand cities. Labor movements, socialism, and colonial raw-material hunger travel together.

The “industrial age” is uneven: Lancashire and Lagos do not share the same clock.

Chapter 4 of 4

Meanings people assigned

Enlightenment perfectibility, Romantic medieval nostalgia, millenarian awakenings, and scientific racism all offer rival stories of what the age meant.

Why it still matters: our institutions — corporations, nation-states, human rights language — are early-modern and industrial inventions still under renovation.
