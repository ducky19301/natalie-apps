1592–1598

Yi Sun-sin

Korea · Joseon dynasty seas · Turtle ships and the Imjin War

When Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s Japan invaded Korea in 1592, Admiral Yi Sun-sin’s naval victories — using panokseon warships and the famous geobukseon turtle ships — cut Japanese supply lines and became a cornerstone of Korean historical memory.

Categories: Science & invention

Chapter 1 of 6

Invasion

Japanese armies surged through the peninsula toward Ming China. Joseon land forces reeled; control of the sea suddenly decided whether invaders could eat and reinforce.

Chapter 2 of 6

Turtle ship

The geobukseon featured a covered top (often described with iron elements in later tradition), dragon prow, and heavy cannon. It was one tool among disciplined gunnery and local knowledge of currents.

Chapter 3 of 6

Hansan and beyond

Victories such as Hansando (1592) used crane-wing formations to smash Japanese fleets. Yi’s war diary, the Nanjung ilgi, records relentless operational detail.

Chapter 4 of 6

Court intrigue

Rivalries led to Yi’s imprisonment and demotion. His replacement’s disaster forced reinstatement — a dramatic arc Koreans still recount.

Chapter 5 of 6

Last battle

At Noryang in 1598 Yi was killed by a bullet as allied Joseon–Ming forces finished the naval war. His death sealed a heroic narrative.

Chapter 6 of 6

Why it still matters

Yi is a national hero in Korea and a case study in logistics, coastal defense, and leadership under political sabotage.

Why it still matters — not as nostalgia, but as unfinished argument.

Further reading vibe

Yi’s War Diary translations; Samuel Hawley on the Imjin War; museum ships and replicas in Korea.
