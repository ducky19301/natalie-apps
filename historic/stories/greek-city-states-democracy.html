Greek City-States & Athenian Democracy

Poleis · Athens · lesser-known angles

The polis was a face-to-face political animal — walls, agora, gods, and rivalries. Athens’s democracy still inspires, but its exclusions and imperial behavior belong in the same paragraph as its speeches.

Chapter 1 of 4

Many Greeces, not one Greece

Hundreds of poleis experimented with oligarchy, tyranny, and democracy. Sparta’s dual kingship and perioikoi, Corinth’s commerce, Thebes’s later hegemony — Athens was loud, not alone.

Colonies from Black Sea to Sicily exported civic forms and created new ethnic mixtures. “Greekness” was argued, not given.

Chapter 2 of 4

How Athenian democracy worked (and for whom)

Adult male citizens could sit in the Assembly, serve on huge juries, and hold allotted offices. Ostracism and accountability procedures tried to blunt charismatic danger.

Women, metics (resident foreigners), and a large enslaved population sustained the leisure of politics. Democracy for some rested on unfreedom for others — a fact ancient critics and modern readers both notice.

Chapter 3 of 4

Empire in democratic clothing

The Delian League’s treasury move to Athens and coercive tribute financed monuments and fleets. Thucydides’ Melian Dialogue remains a cold lesson in power speaking frankly.

Lesser-known: deme identity, lottery machines (kleroteria), and pay for public service that widened participation among poorer citizens.

Chapter 4 of 4

After the golden brochure

Defeat in the Peloponnesian War, the Thirty Tyrants, and later Macedonian domination did not erase civic habits. Hellenistic cities kept councils under kings.

Why it still matters: modern democracies inherit Athenian words more than Athenian institutions — useful inspiration, dangerous nostalgia.

Sortition and slavery shared the same century; honesty requires both.
